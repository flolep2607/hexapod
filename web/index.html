The learner starts as an exact copy of the hand-tuned gait on the right, then searches for something better on MIXED. Each iteration samples random directions in parameter space, runs the gait forward and backward along each on the centroidal plant (not the Rapier robot on screen), and steps toward the directions that scored best. There is no gradient and no neural network — 489 parameters: six gait scalars, one phase offset per leg, two stance trims, and a 19×25 linear feedback matrix mapping what the machine senses onto what it does with its legs, its gait clock, its jump, and its steering.

What this is

A legged-locomotion simulator with a policy-search trainer, written in Rust and compiled to WebAssembly. Four legs to ten; six is the default. The browser only draws; every piece of kinematics, physics and learning runs in the wasm module.

The simulator

The live robot is Rapier: a chassis, three revolute joints per leg (eighteen on a hexapod), and Coulomb friction at the feet. Gait, analytic 3-DOF IK and the servo torque-speed cap are written here; the engine is not. Motors track the IK solution with the stall torque of the servo you picked. That is the first milestone — standing on a plane, then a tripod gait — and it already uses the engine.

A centroidal rigid-body model still runs the learner. A Rapier step is too expensive for in-page ARS (the centroidal tick is about seven microseconds). Training therefore keeps the fast plant; the dashboard shows the articulated one. Both are driven by the same gait, IK and servo numbers.

The chassis carries linear and angular momentum; it cannot change speed faster than traction allows, it coasts when a foot slips, and its own acceleration throws the centre of mass around — braking hard pitches the mass forward over the toes.

Contact is Coulomb friction: a stance foot transmits at most μ·N, and past that it skids and its contact point moves in the world, corrupting the support polygon for every tick after. Loose rubble grips about two thirds of what firm ground does.

Joints are driven by a real servo — the one selected in the control rail — along its torque-speed line: full speed at no load, zero speed at stall, and past stall the joint back-drives, the leg folds and the chassis sags. The gait you train is a gait for that servo.

Leg mass is real and it is not small — on a machine this size the legs are about half of it. A swinging leg holds itself up against gravity and has to be accelerated and stopped, its joints pay for both in torque, and the reaction goes back into the chassis and eats the same friction budget as everything else. Swing used to be free.

The frame is parametric: four legs to ten, in pairs. Nothing in the simulator knows it is a hexapod.

The course is fenced by two invisible walls at the corridor edges, and some of what is inside cannot be climbed — a slalom wall is nearly twice the length of a leg. Both are the same obstruction test, and the legs answer it too: a femur that would enter a wall stops the step the same way the chassis does. Hitting one takes the velocity component into it and keeps the component along it, so a machine that arrives square stops and one that arrives at an angle slides along and gets round; a step aimed at somewhere a leg cannot reach is deflected to the nearest ground that it can.

What the centroidal learner still approximates, stated plainly: links are rigid, contact is resolved once per tick rather than by Rapier's impulse solver, and the leg-inertia terms are the diagonal ones — each joint sees the mass below it, without the off-diagonal coupling or the Coriolis terms of a full mass matrix. The live view is the articulated engine; the learner stays cheap enough to run in the page.

The learner

Augmented Random Search (Mania, Guy & Recht, 2018), the V2-t variant: finite-difference estimates along random directions, scaled by the spread of the returns, using only the best-scoring directions, over a linear policy with normalised observations. Derivative free, so the simulator never has to be differentiable.

The update comes from the spread of returns across sampled directions, so the population it needs grows with the parameter count — and the parameter count grew by seventy per cent when the policy gained a forward scan and somewhere to go. Run it at eight directions and it still converges, to a worse policy: the walk-to-run duty trend flattens out and speed tracking lands behind the hand-tuned gait. More iterations do not fix that. More directions do, which is why the default is sixteen.

The reward contains no distance term at all. The task is to hold a commanded speed, and the command is sampled fresh for every rollout and fed to the policy as an observation, so there is no single speed to specialise on. An early version rewarded raw distance and the optimiser correctly pinned cycle time and stride to their bounds; the version after that hard-coded one cruise speed, which only moved the specialisation somewhere less visible. Beyond tracking it pays for mechanical work in joules, foot skid in metres, terrain clipped by a swinging foot, and time spent asking a servo for more torque than it has.

Every course carries a route, and one action is steering. The policy sees the bearing and range to the next waypoint, where it sits between the two walls, and a six-point forward scan of the ground one to three metres ahead — which is the only thing it knows about terrain it has not reached, and far enough ahead to turn on. On the open courses the route runs straight down the middle and none of it matters. On the slalom, Follow route yaws toward each gate so the open-loop gait weaves instead of walking into the first wall; the policy's steer action is a residual on top of that. The walls themselves cannot be stepped or jumped: they are 1.8 m, as tall as a fully stretched leg.

Three of the actions scale cycle time, stride and duty factor online, every tick. That is how any legged controller changes speed, and it is where the interesting behaviour shows up: given a range of commands to serve, the policy learns to raise its duty factor when walking slowly and drop it when running — the walk-to-run transition, which nobody put in the reward.

A seventh action is jump. Above a threshold it crouches, pushes and lifts every foot at once — the only way this machine leaves the ground, because a walking duty cannot. The seeded weight is zero, so a walking rollout does not jump. On the JUMP course the trenches are wider than a stride and the platforms sit behind a gap: the seed jumps when it sees a pit, keeps its forward speed, and has to land without stripping the servos. The command is still a speed.

Simulation

Empty-field drill

Empty plane. Five legs hold and only react. The red one relocates. Grey X is the plant: if a foot leaves it, grip lost. Walk is the same plant, one foot at a time, along the command.

Policy

Train a policy to enable the comparison.

Frame

Four legs to ten, in pairs. The chassis grows, the policy is reshaped around the new observation and action counts, and anything learned for the old frame is discarded. A quadruped starts on the crawl: a trot stands on two diagonal feet, which is dynamically stable and not statically stable, and this simulator judges stability statically.

Gait pattern

Footfalls as they happen, not the schedule they were meant to follow: each row is one leg over the last four seconds, dark where the foot was down and darker the more weight it carried. Everything under it — cycle time, duty per leg, how many legs are ever in the air at once, and which named pattern that adds up to — is measured from the same recording, so a learned policy that invents a coordination nobody has a name for reads as IRREGULAR rather than as whatever it started from.

Commanded speed

The reward is speed tracking, not distance, and the command is an input to the policy. Move it and watch the learned gait change its cycle time, stride and duty factor to keep up — the hand-tuned one cannot, because it has no feedback layer.

The machine

The servo's torque-speed line drives the joints. Pick an undersized one and the legs give way under load and the chassis sags. Changing it changes the robot, so anything already learned is discarded.

Navigation

Every course carries a route, and the corridor is fenced by two invisible walls. With the autopilot on, the machine yaws toward the next waypoint and the policy's steering action is a residual on top of that — on the slalom that is the only way past, because the walls are taller than a leg can reach and cannot be jumped. Touch the turn keys and steering hands back to you until you let go. Orbit, Top and Side lock the camera (keys 1 / 2 / 3); drag the stage to take orbit back. Obstacles are coloured by kind, and a solid the chassis or a link is inside turns red.
